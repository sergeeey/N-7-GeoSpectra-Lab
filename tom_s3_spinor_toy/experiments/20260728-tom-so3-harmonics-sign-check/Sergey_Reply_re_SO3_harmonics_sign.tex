\noindent\emph{This note only addresses the $SO(3)$ / $l=1$ harmonic
sign--transpose question raised in your PDF. It is a warm-up calculation on
$S^2$, isolating the operator/generator convention issue before we move to
the full $S^3$ spinor-harmonic construction --- it does not yet say
anything about that larger question.}

\section{The two comparisons, side by side}

Your own check computes, for a candidate abstract matrix $T'_i$ and the
combined column of basis functions $v = (\psi_{1,1};\psi_{1,0};\psi_{1,-1})$,
the ordinary matrix--vector product $T'_i \cdot v$, and compares it
entrywise to $(\hat L_i\psi_{1,1};\,\hat L_i\psi_{1,0};\,\hat L_i\psi_{1,-1})$.

That is a well-defined thing to compute, but it is a \emph{different}
operation from asking: ``what matrix, in the basis
$\{\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1}\}$, actually represents $\hat L_i$?''

The second question is answered like this: apply $\hat L_i$ to
\emph{one basis function at a time}, and expand the result back in the same
basis:
\[
  \hat L_i\,\psi_{1,m} \;=\; \sum_{m'} M_{m'm}\,\psi_{1,m'} ,
\]
so that column $m$ of the matrix $M$ is exactly the (unique) set of
coefficients needed to write $\hat L_i\psi_{1,m}$ back in the basis. This
$M$ is the honest matrix representation of $\hat L_i$.

If $(T'_i\cdot v)_{row}$ happens to equal $\hat L_i\psi_{1,\,\mathrm{row}}$
for every row, that tells you $T'_i$ and $M$ are related by a
\textbf{transpose}: $(T'_i \cdot v)_{row} = \sum_{col} (T'_i)_{row,col}\,\psi_{1,col}$
uses the \emph{rows} of $T'_i$, whereas the honest construction of $M$
above reads off the \emph{columns}. For a diagonal or otherwise symmetric
matrix the row and column pictures coincide, which is exactly why your
$\hat L_z = T'_z$ check gave a clean match --- $T'_z=\mathrm{diag}(1,0,-1)$
is trivially symmetric. It is not a coincidence that the one clean
component was the diagonal one.

\section{Building $M$ three separate computational routes}

I built the honest matrix $M_x$ (and $M_y$, $M_z$) three separate ways, to
make sure the conclusion did not depend on which technique was used ---
these are three computational cross-checks rather than three fully
independent derivations, since all three use the same operators, basis
functions, and conventions throughout.

\textbf{Method A --- direct solve.} Apply $\hat L_x$ to each of
$\psi_{1,1}=\sin\theta\,e^{i\phi}$, $\psi_{1,0}=\cos\theta$,
$\psi_{1,-1}=\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}$ in turn, then solve the linear system
for the coefficients that re-express each result in the same basis
(matching the $e^{i\phi}$, constant, and $e^{-i\phi}$ parts separately).

\textbf{Method B --- coefficient extraction.} Same idea, but the
coefficients are extracted by evaluating the residual at a few sample
values of $\phi$ and solving directly, rather than a general symbolic
solve --- a cheap cross-check on Method A.

\textbf{Method C --- inner-product (bra--ket) projection.} The cleanest of
the three, and the one I would recommend as the standard going forward:
\[
  M_{m'm} \;=\; \frac{\langle \psi_{1,m'} \mid \hat L_i \mid \psi_{1,m}\rangle}
                     {\langle \psi_{1,m'} \mid \psi_{1,m'}\rangle},
  \qquad
  \langle f \mid g\rangle := \int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^{2\pi} \overline{f}\,g\,\sin\theta\;d\phi\,d\theta .
\]
This is exactly the ordinary quantum-mechanical way of reading off a
matrix element, and it sidesteps the row/column ambiguity entirely, since
each entry of $M$ is defined by its own independent integral. The simple
form above (dividing by $\langle\psi_{1,m'}\mid\psi_{1,m'}\rangle$ alone)
relies on $\{\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1}\}$ being an orthogonal set,
which they are here; for a non-orthogonal basis one would need the full
Gram-matrix inversion, $M=G^{-1}A$ with $A_{m'm}=\langle\psi_{m'}|\hat
L_i|\psi_m\rangle$ and $G_{m'm}=\langle\psi_{m'}|\psi_m\rangle$.

All three methods gave, for your specific choice
$\chi_{1,1}=\chi_{1,-1}=\sin\theta$, $\chi_{1,0}=\cos\theta$, exactly:
\[
  M_x \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\tfrac12 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 & 0\end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  M_y \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \tfrac{i}{2} & 0 \\ -i & 0 & -i \\ 0 & \tfrac{i}{2} & 0\end{pmatrix},
  \quad
  M_z \;=\; \mathrm{diag}(1,0,-1).
\]

Comparing these directly to your own $T'_x, T'_y, T'_z$ (equations 19--21
of your note):
\[
  M_x = -\big(T'_x\big)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad
  M_y = +\big(T'_y\big)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad
  M_z = T'_z ,
\]
each an exact identity (zero residual), independent of which of the three
methods above produced $M$.

\section{Why $y$ and $z$ looked clean and $x$ did not --- the real reason}

A clarification on terminology first: $M$ above is the matrix acting on
\emph{coefficient} columns (if $\Psi=(\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1})$ is
treated as the basis itself and a state is a coefficient vector $c$ in that
basis, $M$ sends $c\mapsto Mc$). Your own comparison instead treats
$\Psi$ as the column being acted on directly, so it tests $M^{\mathsf T}$,
not $M$ --- consistent with Section 1.

I initially described the match for $y,z$ as ``coincidental,'' which was
sloppy and not quite right. Since $\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1}$ are
linearly independent, an exact match across all three rows is a genuine
matrix identity, not a numerical accident tied to one test vector. The
real explanation is more structural, and traces back to your own $S$
matrix directly.

Identify $\psi_{1,1}=x+iy$, $\psi_{1,0}=z$, $\psi_{1,-1}=x-iy$ (exactly,
with $x=\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y=\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z=\cos\theta$ --- no
extra normalization needed). Applying $\hat L_i$ to the plain Cartesian
triple $(x,y,z)$ gives, for \emph{all three} generators at once:
\[
  \hat L_i\,(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T} \;=\; -\,T_i\,(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T},
\]
using your \emph{original} (un-diagonalized) generators $T_x,T_y,T_z$ from
equations (14)--(16). This is a single, uniform sign --- the standard fact
that a differential operator realizing a rotation of \emph{functions}
carries the opposite sign to the same rotation acting on the coordinates
themselves. It is not itself the source of the asymmetry between $x$ and
$y,z$.

The asymmetry comes from matching your $S$-diagonalized ordering to the
$(\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1})$ ordering you actually compare
against. Writing $X=(x,y,z)^{\mathsf T}$, direct computation gives
$S X = (-\psi_{1,-1},\,\psi_{1,0},\,-\psi_{1,1})^{\mathsf T}$ --- the
\emph{same} three functions as $\Psi=(\psi_{1,1},\psi_{1,0},\psi_{1,-1})^{\mathsf T}$,
but reordered and with two sign flips. That reordering is exactly the
matrix
\[
  C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&1&0\\-1&0&0\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad C^2=I,\qquad \Psi = C\,S X .
\]
Combining the uniform sign above with this reordering gives, for every
generator at once,
\[
  \hat L_i\,\Psi \;=\; -\,C\,T'_i\,C^{-1}\,\Psi ,
\]
which I have checked directly matches $\hat L_i\Psi$ exactly for
$i=x,y,z$. The point is what $C$-conjugation does to each $T'_i$:
\[
  C\,T'_x\,C^{-1} = T'_x \quad(\text{unchanged}), \qquad
  C\,T'_y\,C^{-1} = -T'_y, \qquad
  C\,T'_z\,C^{-1} = -T'_z .
\]
Combined with the uniform ``$-1$'' from the active/passive sign, $x$ picks
up $(-1)(+1)=-1$ relative to $T'_x$, while $y,z$ pick up
$(-1)(-1)=+1$ relative to $T'_y,T'_z$ --- exactly reproducing
$M_x=-(T'_x)^{\mathsf T}$, $M_y=+(T'_y)^{\mathsf T}$, $M_z=T'_z$. The
$x$-generator is singled out because $C$ (forced by the specific ordering
and sign convention you used for $\psi_{1,\pm1}$) happens to commute with
$T'_x$ but anti-commute with $T'_y$ and $T'_z$ --- a systematic consequence
of the basis reordering, not an accident of specific numerical entries.

In short: your arithmetic in equations (1)--(29) is correct throughout --- I
rechecked every one of them independently and found no error --- and the
$-1$ on $x$ alone has this precise, traceable origin, not an arbitrary one.

\section{One small, unrelated thing, for completeness}
